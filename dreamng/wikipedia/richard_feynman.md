# Richard Feynman

**Richard Phillips Feynman** (/ˈfaɪnmən/; May 11, 1918 – February 15, 1988) was an American theoretical physicist, author, and professor of physics at the California Institute of Technology (Caltech). He is widely known for his work in quantum mechanics, where he developed the path integral formulation and the Feynman diagrams, revolutionary pictures of particle interactions. He also made significant contributions to superfluidity in supercooled liquid helium, the physics of particle accelerators, and the physics of the weak force. His work in particle physics led to the development of the parton model.

Feynman was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1965 for his work in quantum electrodynamics (QED), which he shared with Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga. He is also known for his work in particle physics, for proposing the preponderance of matter over antimatter, for his work in superfluidity, for his work in particle accelerators, and for his work in the weak force.

## Early life and education
Feynman was born in Queens, New York, the son of Lucille (née Mottelson) and Richard Philip Feynman. He had a younger brother, John, who was a musician. His father was of Jewish descent from Russia and his mother was of Polish-Jewish descent from Belarus. He was not particularly intelligent as a child, but his parents encouraged him to be curious and to explore the world around him. Feynman attended Far Rockaway High School, where he was the top student in his class. He then attended the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), where he earned a bachelor's degree in physics in 1939. He continued his studies at Princeton University, where he earned his Ph.D. in physics in 1942. His doctoral thesis was on the principle of least action in terms of particle theory.

## Manhattan Project and Los Alamos
During World War II, Feynman worked on the Manhattan Project at the Los Alamos National Laboratory in New Mexico. He was part of a group of young physicists who were tasked with developing the atomic bomb. Feynman was known for his curiosity and his ability to solve complex problems. He also developed a reputation for being a bit of a troublemaker, often picking locks and playing pranks on his colleagues. Despite his youthful demeanor, Feynman made significant contributions to the project, particularly in the area of neutron diffusion.

## Quantum electrodynamics and the Nobel Prize
After the war, Feynman worked at the University of Wisconsin and then at Johns Hopkins University. In 1945, he returned to Caltech, where he remained for the rest of his career. It was at Caltech that he developed his most famous work: quantum electrodynamics (QED). QED is the theory of the interaction of light and matter. Feynman's approach to QED was radically different from that of his contemporaries. He developed the path integral formulation, which describes the behavior of particles in terms of all possible paths they could take. He also developed the Feynman diagrams, a pictorial representation of the mathematical expressions describing the behavior of subatomic particles. These diagrams revolutionized the field of particle physics and are still used today.

Feynman's work on QED earned him the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1965, which he shared with Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga.

## Other contributions to physics
Feynman made many other contributions to physics. He developed the parton model, which describes the internal structure of protons and neutrons. He also made significant contributions to the understanding of superfluidity, the physics of particle accelerators, and the weak force. In 1986, he was appointed to the Rogers Commission, which investigated the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster. Feynman's famous demonstration of the O-ring failure using a clamp and a glass of ice water became one of the most iconic moments in the history of scientific investigations.

## Education and popularization of science
Feynman was also known for his ability to explain complex scientific concepts to the general public. He wrote several popular books, including "Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!" and "What Do You Care What Other People Think?", which are autobiographical accounts of his life and career. He also wrote "QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter", which explains the principles of quantum electrodynamics to a non-technical audience. Feynman was a gifted teacher and his lectures at Caltech were recorded and published as "The Feynman Lectures on Physics", which are still widely used today.

## Personal life and controversies
Feynman was married twice. His first wife was Arline Greenbaum, who died of tuberculosis in 1945. He had two children with her. His second wife was Mary Louise Bell, with whom he had one child. Feynman was known for his unconventional lifestyle and his interest in art, music, and biology. He was also known for his infidelities and his interest in sexual exploration. In 1988, Feynman died of pancreatic cancer at the age of 69.

## Legacy
Feynman is widely regarded as one of the most important physicists of the 20th century. His work on QED and the development of the Feynman diagrams revolutionized the field of particle physics. He was also a gifted teacher and a popularizer of science. His legacy continues to inspire generations of physicists and science enthusiasts.
