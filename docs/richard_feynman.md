# Richard Feynman

## Early Life and Education

Richard Phillips Feynman (May 11, 1918 – February 15, 1988) was an American theoretical physicist known for his work in quantum mechanics, quantum electrodynamics, and the physics of superfluidity. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1965 for his contributions to quantum electrodynamics.

Born in Queens, New York, Feynman showed an early aptitude for mathematics and science. His father, Melville Feynman, was a uniform salesman who encouraged his son's curiosity and analytical thinking. His mother, Lucille Phillips, was a homemaker.

Feynman attended the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), where he earned his bachelor's degree in 1939. He then pursued his PhD at Princeton University, completing his doctoral work in 1942 under the supervision of John Archibald Wheeler.

## Manhattan Project

During World War II, Feynman worked on the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos National Laboratory. Despite his youth, he became a group leader and made significant contributions to the theoretical calculations needed for the development of the atomic bomb. He was known for his ability to solve complex problems and his unconventional approach to physics.

## Academic Career

After the war, Feynman taught at Cornell University before moving to the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) in 1951, where he remained for the rest of his career. At Caltech, he became known for his exceptional teaching abilities and his popular undergraduate physics lectures, which were later published as "The Feynman Lectures on Physics."

## Quantum Electrodynamics

Feynman's most significant contribution to physics was his development of quantum electrodynamics (QED). He introduced Feynman diagrams, a pictorial representation of the mathematical expressions governing the behavior of subatomic particles. This work, done independently by Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga, earned them the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1965.

## Other Contributions

Feynman made contributions to many areas of physics, including:
- The theory of superfluidity in liquid helium
- Weak interactions and the parton model in particle physics
- Quantum computing concepts
- Nanotechnology and molecular machines

## Challenger Disaster

In 1986, Feynman served on the Rogers Commission investigating the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster. He famously demonstrated the cause of the failure by showing how the O-ring seals became brittle in cold temperatures, using a simple experiment with ice water and a sample of the O-ring material.

## Personal Life and Legacy

Feynman was known for his colorful personality, curiosity, and unconventional approach to life. He enjoyed playing bongo drums, painting, and solving lock puzzles. He was married three times and had one daughter.

Feynman passed away from cancer on February 15, 1988, at the age of 69. His legacy continues through his scientific contributions, his influential lectures, and his popular books including "Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!" and "What Do You Care What Other People Think?"
